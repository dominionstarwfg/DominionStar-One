The fundamentals

What is a revocable living trust?

A revocable living trust is a legal arrangement established during life to hold and manage assets for named beneficiaries. The person establishing the trust commonly serves as an initial trustee, retains control over trust assets, and can change or revoke the trust while legally capable.

Continuity during incapacity

A named successor trustee may manage property held in the trust if the current trustee cannot act, subject to the trust's terms.

Transfer outside probate

Assets properly titled in the trust may transfer under the trust terms without a probate proceeding for those assets.

Privacy considerations

Trust administration can be more private than probate court filings, although some records or disclosures may still be required.

Coordinated legacy planning

Trusts should be coordinated with wills, powers of attorney, beneficiary designations, and insurance policies.

Living trust vs. will

Will

Directs distribution of probate assets at death and can nominate guardians for minor children. A will commonly requires probate to administer assets that pass under it.

Revocable living trust

Can govern management of assets while alive and their distribution after death. Probate avoidance generally depends on actually transferring appropriate assets into the trust.

Important: Many comprehensive estate plans use both a trust and a pour-over will. A trust does not automatically replace every estate-planning document.

Trust funding matters

Creating and signing a trust is not the same as funding it. Depending on the asset, funding may require changing title, preparing deeds, or coordinating beneficiary designations. An attorney and relevant financial institutions can help determine the correct steps.

What a revocable trust does not automatically do

  • Eliminate federal or state estate taxes.
  • Shield the grantor's assets from their own creditors.
  • Override all beneficiary designations or account ownership rules.
  • Guarantee that every asset avoids probate.
  • Replace legal guidance tailored to state law and family circumstances.

Questions families often ask

Does everyone need a living trust?

No. Needs vary by assets, state law, privacy preferences, family circumstances, and cost. An estate-planning attorney can compare alternatives.

Does a trust protect my life insurance proceeds?

Life insurance proceeds usually follow the policy's beneficiary designation. Naming a trust as a beneficiary can have significant legal, tax, and administrative consequences and should be reviewed with qualified professionals.

Can I change a revocable trust later?

Generally, yes, while legally capable, subject to applicable law and the trust's terms.

Is this legal advice or trust preparation?

No. DominionStar provides general education here, not legal representation, individualized estate-planning advice, or trust drafting.

Prepare for an informed conversation

Consider the assets you own, who should manage them if you cannot, the people you want to benefit, existing beneficiary designations, and whether you have current estate-planning documents.
